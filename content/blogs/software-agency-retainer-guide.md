A **software agency retainer** makes sense when a live product needs recurring improvement, maintenance, monitoring, or access to several technical skills. It is less suitable when there is no predictable stream of valuable work or no shared prioritization process.

## What can a retainer include?

Depending on the agreement, ongoing work may cover:

- Security and dependency updates.
- Bug investigation and fixes.
- Performance and availability monitoring.
- Small product improvements.
- Analytics reviews and experiments.
- Technical advice and roadmap planning.
- Incident response and release support.

Define whether unused capacity expires, rolls over, or can be exchanged for planned work. Also distinguish normal requests from urgent incidents.

## How is a retainer different from a project?

A project targets a defined outcome with a start and finish. A retainer reserves recurring capability or service levels. It should not become a vague monthly fee; the agency still needs a prioritized queue, visible output, and reporting.

## What should the contract clarify?

Document included hours or capacity, team roles, response and resolution targets, communication channels, supported environments, exclusions, access, security duties, and termination terms. Specify who can approve work and what happens when demand exceeds capacity.

## How should value be measured?

Use outcome and service metrics such as release frequency, incident volume, resolution time, uptime, performance, conversion, or manual hours removed. Hours consumed can help with planning but do not prove value by themselves.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do new websites need a retainer?

Not always. Every site needs an owner for updates, backups, security, and failures, but that responsibility may sit internally, with hosting, or with an agency.

### How large should the monthly commitment be?

Base it on recent demand and criticality. Start small, review actual usage, and adjust with evidence.

### Can strategy and development share one retainer?

Yes, if allocation and priorities are transparent. Protect enough delivery capacity so planning does not consume the entire agreement.
