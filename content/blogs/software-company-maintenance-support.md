**Software company maintenance and support** should keep a live system secure, available, understandable, and useful as technology and business needs change. A good agreement separates preventive maintenance, incident response, and new product development.

## What belongs in maintenance?

Typical maintenance work includes dependency and security updates, bug fixes, performance review, backup checks, infrastructure changes, compatibility testing, and documentation updates. Preventive work reduces the chance that small neglected issues become expensive emergencies.

## What belongs in support?

Support covers how issues are reported, classified, investigated, communicated, and resolved. Define severity levels with examples. A login failure for one user and a failed payment flow for every customer should not receive the same priority.

## Which service levels should be documented?

Clarify:

- Supported hours and emergency coverage.
- Response targets by severity.
- Restoration or workaround targets where realistic.
- Communication channels and escalation contacts.
- Included systems and environments.
- Monitoring and alert ownership.
- Maintenance windows.
- Monthly capacity and overage rates.

A response target means investigation begins; it is not automatically a guaranteed resolution time.

## Who should own access and knowledge?

The client should retain appropriate access to source control, hosting, domains, monitoring, analytics, and vendor accounts. Keep setup steps, architecture context, deployment procedures, and incident notes current so the system is not dependent on one person.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much maintenance does software need?

It depends on usage, integrations, risk, and rate of change. Review actual incidents, updates, and roadmap demand each quarter.

### Are new features included in support?

Only if the agreement says so. Many teams reserve separate capacity for improvements.

### Can another company maintain existing software?

Yes, after a technical and security assessment. Plan a structured handover and address critical risks before promising service levels.
