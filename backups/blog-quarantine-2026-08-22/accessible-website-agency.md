An **accessible website agency** should integrate accessibility into research, design, content, development, and testing. An automated score or last-minute plugin is not a complete accessibility process.

## What should the agency do?

Expected practices include:

- Use clear structure and semantic HTML.
- Design sufficient contrast and visible focus states.
- Support keyboard and assistive-technology use.
- Label controls and communicate errors clearly.
- Provide text alternatives for meaningful media.
- Avoid interactions that depend on one sense or precise motion.
- Test zoom, reflow, reduced motion, and common screen sizes.
- Give editors guidance for accessible content.

The appropriate standard and legal obligations depend on the organization and jurisdiction. Obtain qualified legal advice where required.

## How should accessibility be tested?

Automation can find some issues quickly, but manual review is essential. Ask about keyboard testing, screen-reader checks, component states, forms, modals, menus, media, and real-user research where feasible.

Accessibility acceptance criteria should appear in design and engineering tickets rather than in one audit after development.

## Which claims should make you cautious?

Be wary of “100% compliant” guarantees, overlay-only solutions, or a proposal that never mentions manual testing. Accessibility is an ongoing quality practice because content, integrations, and code continue changing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is accessibility only for government websites?

No. Accessible design helps people with permanent, temporary, and situational disabilities and often improves usability for everyone.

### Can an existing site be improved?

Yes. Start with critical journeys and high-impact barriers, then establish a prioritized remediation plan.

### Does accessibility harm visual design?

No. Constraints can guide clearer, more inclusive design without making every site look the same.
