A strong **website agency brief** explains the business context, audience, desired outcomes, and known constraints. It gives the agency enough clarity to ask better questions without pretending every solution is already decided.

## What should the brief contain?

Include:

1. A short organization and offer overview.
2. Why the project is happening now.
3. Primary audiences and their needs.
4. Problems with the current website.
5. Desired actions and measurable outcomes.
6. Essential pages, content, and features.
7. Integrations and technical constraints.
8. Brand assets and accessibility needs.
9. Internal stakeholders and decision owner.
10. Budget range, target date, and procurement process.

Attach analytics, user research, content inventories, brand guidelines, and relevant system documentation when available.

## How detailed should the feature list be?

Describe capabilities and user outcomes rather than prescribing every interface. “Customers need to reschedule appointments” is more useful than dictating a specific button and database design before discovery.

Label items as essential, desirable, or future ideas. This helps the agency protect the core outcome when budget or timing changes.

## Who should approve the brief?

The people responsible for business results, content, sales or service workflows, technology, and compliance should review the relevant sections. Appoint one accountable decision-maker to resolve conflicting feedback.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should the brief include competitor websites?

Yes, if you explain what is relevant about them. A list of links without reasons encourages superficial imitation.

### Do we need a final sitemap?

No. Provide your current thinking and content inventory; information architecture can be refined through discovery.

### Should we disclose the budget?

A range helps the agency recommend a realistic scope and avoids proposals designed for completely different investment levels.
